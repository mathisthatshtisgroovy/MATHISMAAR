### Text by Nadine Botha

**Syrinxes**  
_Anti-Atlas of Bodies Stuffed with Feathers_

A chance encounter between a singing chaffinch, a factory worker’s whistle and the electromagnetic emissions surrounding ASML led Mathis Dabbarh Marsepoil to explore how culture is transmitted across human, technological and more-than-human worlds. Following migratory bird routes from southern Morocco, where his roots lie, to his current home in the Netherlands, Marsepoil spent five months recording birdsong, radio transmissions, electromagnetic fields and spoken language; meeting conservationists, scientists, musicians and craftspeople; and searching archival collections and digital memory systems.

These materials form an immersive installation in which networked signals pass between sculptural bird bodies, sound and electronics, generating an open-ended performance through which visitors move. Bringing together biological, technological and cultural systems of transmission, the installation traces how migration, language and territory carry memory and belonging. It reflects on how colonial legacies in contemporary technology shape what is preserved, what is lost through standardisation or left beyond the archive’s reach.

### Text by Metahaven

Mathis's graduation work is a hyperarchive of bird songs, speaking to ecological thinking in ways that are deep and layered. Rather than through the direct narration of documentary evidence, the work extrapolates the ways in which migrating birds become entrapped in electronic and digital archival forms and circuits. As Mathis writes in this thesis about the migration patterns of the Northern Bald Ibis, "the bird-as-taxon (Aves) resurrects, only to never die again, circulating in eternal metadata traffic." As a physically and sonically rendered act of encryption and descryption, Mathis's work speaks about extinction in a way that bypasses, as of now, most of its current causes and effects, focuing on their data. When reading the sound-based installation and sculptural circuitry, we come to grasp some of the strange melancholy of this work, through nets and contact microphones, while at the same time mourning the absence of the "real" bird. In its focused engagement with cybernetics, Mathis's work ultimately becomes a way of re-reading our data culture and the ways in which it entraps birds in it as forms of digital trace. The thesis document extrapolates convincingly on the living substrate of this data, the complex migratory patterns of the Ibis, and the arduous human lanor involved in trying to sustain a world in which this bird can continue to live. The graduation work, then, takes this environmental narrative into further-layers of abstraction, in a voice and with a sustained sense of energy that situates Mathis' unique voice between research, sound, biology, and computation.
